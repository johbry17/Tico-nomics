The World Bank Group in Costa Rica

A 45-year story of development finance, shifting priorities, and an evolving institutional relationship

Author

Bryan C. Johns

Published

November 12, 2026

A small country with an unusual story

Costa Rica occupies a narrow strip of land between Nicaragua and Panama, home to roughly five million people and an extraordinary diversity of wildlife. It abolished its army in 1948. By the early 2000s it had achieved life expectancy comparable to the United States and near-universal access to education and healthcare, all on an income level that placed it squarely in the middle of the global middle class.

It is also a country that has borrowed substantially from the World Bank for 45 years.

This project examines that borrowing, not as a simple story of a donor and a recipient, but as an evolving relationship between two institutions responding to shifting circumstances, changing ideas about development, and their own strategic priorities. The central question is:

How did the World Bank’s financing relationship with Costa Rica change from 1980 to 2025 — in its scale, its instruments, and its stated priorities — and what does it reveal about the changing practice of international development finance?

The analysis is descriptive. It documents patterns in a 45-year lending record and explores what those patterns might reflect. It does not establish that World Bank lending caused changes in Costa Rica’s economy or society.

Who does what: the World Bank Group

The term “World Bank” can be confusing because it refers to several related but distinct institutions. This project covers three of them.

IBRD — the core lender. The International Bank for Reconstruction and Development makes long-maturity loans to creditworthy middle-income governments. Costa Rica has been an IBRD borrower throughout the study period. This is the main subject of the analysis.

IFC — the private-sector arm. The International Finance Corporation invests in private companies — banks, manufacturers, energy producers, agribusinesses — not governments. It operates separately from the IBRD and reports different kinds of amounts. IFC investment in Costa Rica is substantial and concentrated in financial services and clean energy.

MIGA — the guarantee agency. The Multilateral Investment Guarantee Agency issues political-risk guarantees to private investors. MIGA does not lend money; it backs private investment decisions by absorbing certain political risks. Its Costa Rica portfolio spans tourism, infrastructure, and banking.

Important: These institutions’ numbers cannot be added together. An IBRD loan, an IFC equity investment, and a MIGA guarantee exposure are three different things — different instruments, different financial measures, different kinds of risk. This analysis presents them separately.

A note on IDA: a fourth institution, the International Development Association, provides concessional financing to the world’s poorest countries. Costa Rica graduated long ago; its last IDA credit was in FY1962. The 1980–2025 analysis is entirely IBRD.

Four decades of IBRD lending

Between 1980 and 2025, the IBRD approved 35 projects in Costa Rica with a combined original principal of $4.6B in nominal US dollars — roughly $6.2B in constant 2025 values.

Thirty-four of those projects involved financial commitments; the thirty-fifth (a COVID-19 vaccine operation approved in 2022) was terminated before the loan agreement was signed, so its original principal in the IBRD’s records is $0.

The lending record divides into four broad periods, each shaped by distinct conditions.

The 1980s: debt crisis and structural adjustment

Costa Rica entered the 1980s in economic distress. Between 1979 and 1982, GDP contracted sharply, inflation approached 90%, and the country defaulted on its external debt — the result of commodity price shocks, rising US interest rates, and regional instability. The World Bank, working alongside the International Monetary Fund and USAID, responded with a series of Structural Adjustment Loans.

Structural adjustment remains controversial. The loans provided foreign currency to keep the government solvent; in exchange, Costa Rica agreed to reform its trade policy, reduce state involvement in markets, and restructure public enterprises. SAL I arrived in 1985, SAL II in 1988, and SAL III — extending into the following decade — in 1993.

The Bank also financed more conventional investment alongside adjustment: water supply, petroleum sector assistance, and road rehabilitation. In total, the 1980s saw eight IBRD projects worth $294M in nominal terms — roughly $772M in today’s dollars. Around 70% of that sum, by original principal, went to policy-based adjustment lending; the remainder financed identifiable infrastructure and investment activities.

The 1990s and 2000s: investment and diversification

The 1990s marked a transition away from the most concentrated period of structural adjustment. Through the 1990s and 2000s, the IBRD shifted toward identifiable projects: health system reform, education equity, payment for environmental services, environmental policy, and urban infrastructure. A “catastrophe deferred drawdown option” credit in 2008 provided a disaster-risk buffer that could be called on after a major natural disaster.

A distinctive thread through this period was environmental finance. In 2000, the Bank provided $33 million for the Ecomarkets project, supporting Costa Rica’s pioneering program of payments to landowners for maintaining forest cover. This form of conservation finance — paying for ecosystem services — was largely novel at the time. When the global financial crisis hit in 2008–2009, the Bank approved a $500 million Development Policy Loan for public finance and competitiveness, one of two $500 million operations in the entire portfolio.

The 2010s: two large investments

A decade that might appear lean on the charts was not. It held only two IBRD projects — but they were substantial.

The 2012 Higher Education Improvement loan ($200 million) supported Costa Rica’s public university system. The 2016 Strengthening Universal Health Insurance loan ($420 million) supported the Caja Costarricense del Seguro Social (CCSS), which administers Costa Rica’s universal health care system. The health insurance loan is the single largest investment-type operation in the entire 1980–2025 portfolio, and its inclusion changes the picture of an otherwise quiet decade.

The 2020s: fiscal reform, climate, and pandemic

The 2020–2025 period accounts for 57.9% of all nominal 1980–2025 commitments — twelve projects totalling $2.66 billion. Several forces converged: the COVID-19 crisis, Costa Rica’s long-building fiscal consolidation (anchored by a 2018 tax reform), and the country’s national Decarbonization Plan targeting net-zero emissions by 2050.

The Bank’s response included a series of large Development Policy Loans that linked fiscal reform directly to climate objectives — the Fiscal and Decarbonization DPL series. This pattern — budget support tied simultaneously to fiscal adjustment and green investment — reflects changes in both World Bank strategy and in how Costa Rica frames its own development priorities.

Where the money went (with caveats)

The chart below shows how lending was distributed across sectors — with an important limitation. The 2020–2025 DPL series, which accounts for more than half of that period’s lending by value, does not come with traditional sector percentages, because DPLs operate through government policy rather than specific investment activities. The chart therefore represents only the portion of the portfolio where sector allocations are recorded, covering about 59% of total original principal ($2.7 billion of $4.6 billion).

Within this partial picture — and it is very partial, since recent DPL operations largely lack sector breakdowns — the data shows public administration dominated the portfolio measured by allocated amounts, driven by fiscal management and government systems components in the DPL series. Among the earlier projects with identifiable sector allocations, water and sanitation, education, and agriculture were persistent targets. The 2010s data covers only a single project (the Higher Education loan), so conclusions about that decade’s sector composition are unavoidable limited.

What the Bank said it was doing

The Bank publishes a project abstract for many operations — a statement of objectives and intended activities. The chart below tracks how often certain thematic areas appeared in those abstracts, by period.

This is an analysis of institutional language, not of outcomes or priorities. The 1990s are entirely absent because no abstracts are available for that decade in the data source used here.

The World Bank provides project abstracts for 19 of the 35 operations in this study. Coverage varies substantially by period, with no abstracts available for the 1990s and very limited coverage in the 1980s and 2010s.

With those caveats in mind, the chart does reveal some genuine patterns — but not the ones that are easiest to describe. Infrastructure-related keywords appear across all periods with available abstracts: this mostly tells us that World Bank projects involve infrastructure, which is not surprising. More informative is what changes. Environment and climate language is absent from the two 1980s abstracts, appears in 57% of the 2000s abstracts, dips to zero in the single 2010s abstract, and climbs to 89% of the 2020s abstracts. Governance and fiscal language is likewise absent in the 1980s, then appears consistently in the 2000s onward. Disaster risk language shows up surprisingly in both 1980s abstracts — but the keyword matching picks up any use of the word “risk,” which shows up in structural adjustment documents discussing economic risk, not natural disasters. All of this reflects stated language in project documents, not project effectiveness or actual expenditure.

Costa Rica’s development trajectory

Costa Rica’s improvement on most standard development measures over this period has been substantial. GDP per capita — in current US dollars, unadjusted for inflation — grew from roughly $2,000 in 1980 to $18,900 in 2024. Life expectancy rose from 73 years to 81 years.

Inequality is more complicated. The Gini index reached its historically highest recorded level around 51.8 in 2002 before declining through the 2000s and 2010s. It rose modestly in 2020 during the COVID-19 pandemic, then resumed declining to 45.8 in 2024 — the lowest since the 1990s. A preliminary 2025 estimate from the World Bank API (45.5) suggests continued decline, but that value is not yet confirmed.

What none of this tells us is why these things happened. World Bank lending may have contributed to Costa Rica’s development record — or it may have been incidental to changes driven by Costa Rica’s own institutions, demographic trends, trade patterns, and policy choices. The correlation between lending episodes and development trends is worth noting; establishing causation from these data is not possible.

IFC: private investment

Alongside IBRD lending, IFC has made 23 investments in Costa Rican businesses with indicative own-account amounts totalling approximately $874 million (nominal USD). The portfolio is dominated by financial services — commercial banks, SME lenders, housing finance institutions — consistent with IFC’s mandate in middle-income countries. The largest single non-financial investment is the $100 million Reventazon hydroelectric project (2012).

IFC invests in private entities on commercial terms. Its amounts cannot be compared with IBRD principal or summed to produce a “World Bank Group total.”

MIGA: de-risking private capital

Between 1995 and FY2008, MIGA issued guarantee contracts covering 10 Costa Rica projects (deduplicated). The portfolio spans tourism, banking, power generation, manufacturing, and transportation. MIGA’s financial measure — gross exposure — is the maximum potential liability under issued guarantees, not a loan or a disbursement. The largest guarantee ($158.5 million) covered an investment in the Autopistas del Sol toll road concession (FY2008).

What we can and cannot say

What the evidence supports:

  • IBRD engagement with Costa Rica has been continuous across four decades but has changed character substantially: from adjustment lending in the 1980s, to project investment in health, education, and environment, to large policy-based operations for fiscal reform and decarbonization in the 2020s.
  • Environmental and climate language became prominent in the 2000s and 2020s, absent in the 1980s and in the single available 2010s abstract — consistent with the actual project portfolio shift toward environmental finance after 2000, but caution is needed given sparse abstract coverage.
  • Both 2009 and 2023 saw $500 million IBRD commitments, tied respectively to global financial crisis response and the Fiscal and Decarbonization DPL series.
  • IFC’s presence complements IBRD lending by working in the private sector; the two operate under different mandates and their financial measures are not interchangeable.

What the evidence cannot tell us:

  • Whether World Bank lending caused improvements in Costa Rica’s health, education, or environmental outcomes.
  • Whether structural adjustment conditions of the 1980s and 1990s helped or hurt Costa Rican workers and households.
  • Whether the Bank’s current climate-focused DPL structure is driving Costa Rica’s decarbonization ambitions or largely ratifying ambitions the country was already pursuing.
  • How this portfolio compares to comparable countries, or what it would look like if either party had made different choices.
